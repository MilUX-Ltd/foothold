# UK sources and what each is good for

Start from the source that produced the fact, not a page that repeats it. These are the usual starting points for UK claims. Links are to the organisation's main site; search there for the specific release, then cite the release page itself in the report, not the home page.

## How good is a source?

A rough order, best first:

1. **The original record.** The Act on legislation.gov.uk, the dataset from the body that collected it, the company's own filing, the full study in its journal, the recording of the speech.
2. **An official summary of it.** Guidance on gov.uk, a House of Commons Library briefing, a regulator's published guide.
3. **Independent expert analysis.** Full Fact, the Institute for Fiscal Studies, a university research centre, a professional body.
4. **Quality journalism** that names and links its source. Use it to find the source, then cite the source.
5. **Everything else** (blogs, vendor reports, social posts, AI answers): a lead to follow, never the evidence.

Watch for vendor surveys: a software firm's poll of its own customers is not evidence about "UK businesses". Say who ran it, how many answered and when.

**Official statistics.** In the UK, the Office for Statistics Regulation (part of the UK Statistics Authority) assesses statistics against a Code of Practice. Those that pass are called "accredited official statistics" (the label "National Statistics" was used before late 2023). "Official statistics in development" are newer and less settled. Prefer accredited figures and say which you used.

## By type of claim

| Claim is about | Go to | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Population, economy, jobs, wages, inflation, business counts, trade | Office for National Statistics (ONS), https://www.ons.gov.uk | The main UK statistics producer. Check the release date and the "next release" date on every bulletin. Note geography: some series are UK, some Great Britain, some England and Wales. |
| Small business numbers and size | Department for Business and Trade, "Business population estimates", via https://www.gov.uk | Counts of private-sector businesses, employment and turnover by size band, published yearly. |
| Government policy, schemes, guidance | GOV.UK, https://www.gov.uk | The official version of guidance and policy papers. Check "Last updated" and whether a page is marked withdrawn. |
| What the law says | legislation.gov.uk, https://www.legislation.gov.uk | The text of Acts and regulations. Check the "latest available (revised)" version and any warning that changes are not yet applied. Note the extent: an Act may apply to England only, or England and Wales. |
| Tax rates, thresholds, VAT, payroll | HMRC pages on GOV.UK, https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/hm-revenue-customs | Current rates and thresholds. Rates change each tax year (6 April); check the year the draft means. |
| A UK company: name, number, age, directors, status, accounts | Companies House, https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk | Incorporation date ("established 1998"), whether a company is active or dissolved, officers and filed accounts. A trading history can be older than the company; say which you checked. |
| Briefings on policy, law and statistics in plain terms | House of Commons Library, https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk | Impartial research briefings that cite their sources; good for finding the primary figure and the context. |
| Interest rates, inflation history, money, exchange rates | Bank of England, https://www.bankofengland.co.uk | Bank Rate history, the inflation calculator for comparing money across years, and official exchange-rate data. |
| Data protection, UK GDPR, cookies, marketing emails | Information Commissioner's Office (ICO), https://ico.org.uk | Guidance on what the law requires. Many drafts overstate it ("GDPR bans..."); check the guidance wording. |
| Cyber security claims and advice | National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC), https://www.ncsc.gov.uk | Official guidance, Cyber Essentials, threat reports. |
| Cyber incident figures for UK businesses | "Cyber security breaches survey", via https://www.gov.uk | Yearly survey of UK businesses and charities. Check the year and whether the figure is all businesses or a size band. |
| Financial services firms and claims | FCA Financial Services Register, https://register.fca.org.uk | Whether a firm is authorised, and for what. |
| Charities | Charity Commission register, via https://www.gov.uk | Registration, income, trustees (England and Wales; Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own regulators). |
| Health and medicine | NHS, https://www.nhs.uk; NICE, https://www.nice.org.uk; MHRA via https://www.gov.uk | Public health information, clinical guidance, medicines regulation. Health is devolved: NHS England figures are not UK figures. |
| Claims already checked by others | Full Fact, https://fullfact.org | The UK's independent fact-checking charity. If they have checked a popular claim, read their piece and follow it to the primary source. |
| Research studies | The journal article itself; PubMed, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov; a DOI link (https://doi.org/...) | Read the abstract and the method: sample size, country, date, who paid for it. Check it has not been retracted (journal page, or the Retraction Watch database). |
| What a web page said before | Internet Archive Wayback Machine, https://web.archive.org | Earlier versions of a page, when a source has changed or vanished. |

## Tracing a quote

1. Search for the exact words with the name. Note the earliest dated appearance you can find.
2. Look for the original: the book (with page), the speech transcript, the interview, the recording.
3. Check the specialist quote-tracing sources, such as Quote Investigator (https://quoteinvestigator.com) and the "Disputed" and "Misattributed" sections of Wikiquote (https://en.wikiquote.org). Treat them as leads to the original, and cite the original where they found it.
4. If the earliest appearance is decades after the person's death, or only on quote sites, treat the attribution as unproven. Suggest wording such as "a saying often credited to X".
5. Check the wording too: a real quote is often reworded as it travels.

## Money and units

- Do not convert dollars to pounds and keep the claim. A US market size converted to pounds is still a US figure. Find the UK figure, or keep the original currency and say it is global or US.
- To compare money across years, use the Bank of England inflation calculator and say which years you compared.
- "Billion" means a thousand million. Check the source's unit row in tables ("£ million").
- A rise from 10% to 15% is 5 percentage points, or a 50% increase. Drafts often mix these up.

## Currency of a figure

Ask three questions: when was it published, what period does it cover, and has it been replaced? Many ONS and government series update monthly, quarterly or yearly; the bulletin page says when the next release is due. If a newer figure exists, use it. If the old figure is all there is, date it in the text ("in 2021, ...").
