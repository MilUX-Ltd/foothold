# UK advertising and consumer rules, in plain words

This is a flag list, not legal advice. It explains why certain claims in marketing copy need proof before they go out, so the user can decide what to keep, reword or back up. Rule numbers and guidance change; open the current version before quoting a rule in a report, and link what you opened.

## Who sets the rules

- **The CAP Code** (the UK Code of Non-broadcast Advertising and Direct and Promotional Marketing) is written by the Committee of Advertising Practice and enforced by the **Advertising Standards Authority (ASA)**. It covers adverts in print, email, online ads and, since 2011, marketing claims on a business's own website and its social media accounts. So a LinkedIn post promoting a service counts. Code: https://www.asa.org.uk/codes-and-rulings/advertising-codes/non-broadcast-code.html
- The ASA cannot fine, but it publishes rulings by name, requires the ad to be changed or withdrawn, and can refer persistent cases to Trading Standards. Its published rulings are a good guide to how a rule is applied: https://www.asa.org.uk
- **Consumer protection law** sits behind the code. Since April 2025 the main law on unfair commercial practices, including misleading actions and omissions, is Part 4 of the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, which replaced the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008. The **Competition and Markets Authority (CMA)** can now enforce much of it directly and impose fines. Check the current text on legislation.gov.uk and the CMA's guidance on GOV.UK before citing a section.
- CAP runs a Copy Advice service that will give a view on a specific ad before it runs. Point the user to it for High-harm claims.

## Substantiation: hold the proof first

The CAP Code requires a marketer to hold documentary evidence for any claim a consumer is likely to see as objective, **before** the ad goes out (rule 3.7 at the time of writing). "Documentary evidence" means something you could hand to the ASA: test results, the dataset, the survey with its method, the award certificate.

What this means for the check:

- Any number, ranking, "first", "only", "fastest", "most trusted" or "award-winning" needs proof on file.
- A survey claim ("9 out of 10 customers recommend us") needs the survey: who was asked, how many, when, and the exact question.
- Obvious exaggeration that no reader would take literally ("the friendliest café in the world") is usually allowed; the code calls this puffery. A specific, measurable "best" ("the highest-rated accountant in Bristol on Google") is not puffery and needs proof.
- Evidence must match the claim. A lab test on one product does not support a claim about the range.

## Comparisons with competitors

Comparing yourself with a named or identifiable competitor is allowed if it is fair: like is compared with like, the features compared are material and verifiable, the reader can check the comparison, and the competitor is not denigrated. "Cheaper than X" needs current prices for equivalent products, dated. Implied comparisons ("unlike other agencies, we...") can count if a reader could tell who is meant.

## Environmental claims: the CMA Green Claims Code

The CMA published the Green Claims Code in September 2021 to explain how consumer law applies to environmental claims: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/green-claims-code-making-environmental-claims

It has six principles. A green claim must:

1. be truthful and accurate;
2. be clear and unambiguous;
3. not omit or hide important information;
4. make only fair and meaningful comparisons;
5. consider the full life cycle of the product or service;
6. be substantiated.

The CAP Code has its own section on environmental claims, and the ASA has ruled against many broad claims. In practice:

- Broad words such as "green", "eco-friendly", "sustainable" and "kind to the planet" are hard to prove, because a reader takes them to mean the whole product has little or no impact. Suggest a specific, provable claim instead ("the box is made from 80% recycled card").
- "Recyclable" should mean it can be recycled through facilities most customers can use, such as household collections, not just in theory. Say which parts are recyclable.
- "Carbon neutral" and "net zero" claims need to say what is covered, and whether they rely on offsetting. Readers often assume the business has cut its own emissions.
- "Plastic-free", "biodegradable" and "compostable" need specific evidence (and, for compostable, whether it means home or industrial composting).
- A claim about one part (the packaging) must not suggest the whole product.

## Price claims

Price claims are among the most common causes of complaints and enforcement. Flag:

- **Reference prices** ("was £100, now £60", "RRP £80"): the higher price must be genuine, for example a price the business actually charged for a reasonable period before the sale. A "was" price that was barely used is likely misleading.
- **"Save X%"** and "up to X% off": the saving must be true for a fair share of the range, not one item.
- **"Free"**: the customer should not pay anything beyond the unavoidable cost of responding and delivery. "Free" inside a bundle the customer pays for is risky.
- **Hidden or drip charges**: compulsory fees added later in the buying process. Since April 2025, failing to include unavoidable fees in the headline price is treated as misleading under the 2024 Act; check current CMA guidance for the detail.
- **"From £X"**: a reasonable number of products or bookings should actually be available at that price.
- **VAT**: prices to consumers should normally include VAT. For business buyers, say clearly if a price excludes it.
- **Cheapest or best value**: needs a fair, current comparison (see comparisons above).

## Health claims

Health claims carry tighter rules and higher harm. Flag any claim that a product or service treats, prevents or cures a condition, or improves health:

- Claims that something treats or cures a medical condition generally belong only to licensed medicines and should not appear in ordinary marketing.
- Food and drink health or nutrition claims ("boosts immunity", "high in protein") must come from an authorised list, the [Great Britain nutrition and health claims register](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/great-britain-nutrition-and-health-claims-nhc-register) on GOV.UK, and match its wording and conditions. Northern Ireland follows EU rules on this.
- Wellbeing services (coaching, therapies, supplements) often slip into medical claims. Suggest wording about the experience rather than the outcome.

## How to report these

For each flagged claim, give: what kind of claim it is, the rule or guide it touches (with the link you opened), what evidence would make it safe, and a rewrite that says only what can be proved. Mark it as a flag, not a legal finding.
