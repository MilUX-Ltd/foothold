# The model scale

The same prompt does not suit every model. Think of a scale. At one end, a large model: give it a clear outcome and the reasons, then leave the how to it. At the other end, a small, fast model: give it numbered steps, an example and a fixed format, so it does not go off course. Most everyday models sit in the middle.

Place the user's model by **type**, not brand. Names change every few months; the types do not.

| Size | How to spot it in a picker | How to write the prompt |
|---|---|---|
| **Large** | The vendor's most capable or "thinking", "pro" or "deep" option; often slower, sometimes only on paid plans | Outcome first, with the why, who it is for and the material. Say what good looks like and the limits. Leave the method to the model. Keep it short; don't number every step. |
| **Middle** | The default, "balanced" or everyday option | Outcome and why, then the three or four steps that matter, and the format. An example if the shape or style matters. |
| **Small** | The "fast", "quick", "instant" or "lite" option, or a free plan's default for quick answers | Short, numbered steps, one action each. A worked example of a good result. A fixed format (length, headings, sections). Spell out what not to do. Repeat the one rule that matters most at the end, where SKILL.md rule 4 says. |
| **Unknown** | They aren't sure | Write the small-model version. Tell them it works on a larger model too, just wordier, and show them where to check next time. |

"Auto" or "smart" modes that pick a model for you: treat as unknown, unless the user has picked a model by hand. A name the user half-remembers ("Sonnet, I think?"): treat as unknown too.

## Where to check the model (checked 19 November 2026)

Tell the user where to look, in their tool. If what they see does not match, ask them to read you the name they see, and place it by type.

- **Claude:** the model name next to the send button. Click it to see the list ("More models" shows the rest). Fable and Opus are at the large end, Sonnet in the middle, Haiku at the small end. (Claude Help Center, "Change the model, effort, and thinking settings".)
- **ChatGPT:** the model menu at the top of the chat. The faster "instant" style options are at the small end; the "thinking" and "pro" options at the large end. Plans differ; free plans have fewer choices.
- **Gemini:** tap or click the current model name in the chat box. Gemini lists a fast model, more capable models and its strongest model, plus an Auto option and thinking levels (Low, Medium, High); choosing by hand needs a Google AI plan. (Gemini Apps Help, "About Gemini models".)
- **Microsoft Copilot (the free app):** the "Quick response" button under the prompt opens the modes: Quick response (small end), Think Deeper and Smart (larger end), plus Study and learn and Search. (Microsoft Support, "Conversation modes in Microsoft Copilot".) Microsoft 365 Copilot at work may show a model selector instead; place it by type.

Current examples, for reference only (19 November 2026): Claude Fable 5.2 and Opus 5.5 large, Sonnet 5.5 middle, Haiku 5.5 small (Anthropic, "Models overview"). OpenAI lists GPT-6.2 Astra as its most capable model and GPT-6 Luna as its most efficient (OpenAI, "Models"). Gemini lists Gemini 4 Carbon as its strongest and Gemini 4 Hydrogen as fast. Don't rely on these names: ask what the user sees.

## The same job at both ends

Job: a follow-up email for quotes that went quiet.

**Large model:**

> Write a short, friendly follow-up email I can send to customers who asked us for a building quote and haven't replied after about ten days. We're a family building firm; we lose a lot of work to quotes that just go quiet, and I want people to feel it's easy to come back to us, not chased. Under 120 words, with a subject line, and end with one easy question. No discounts, no pressure. Write it as a draft for me to check. Don't send, post, delete, pay, book or change anything; give me the result here.

**Small model:**

> You are helping a small building firm write one email.
>
> Task: write a follow-up email to a customer who asked for a quote 10 days ago and has not replied.
>
> Steps:
> 1. Thank them for asking us to quote.
> 2. Say the quote is still open, and offer to talk it through or change anything.
> 3. Ask one easy question they can answer in a line, such as whether they'd like a quick call.
>
> Rules:
> - Under 120 words.
> - Friendly and plain. No pressure, no discounts, no deadlines.
> - Use [customer first name] and [job, for example kitchen] as placeholders.
>
> Format:
> Subject: (one line)
> (the email)
>
> Example of a result I liked: [paste one here]
>
> Remember: no pressure, no discounts, no deadlines.
>
> Write it as a draft for me to check. Don't send, post, delete, pay, book or change anything; give me the result here.
