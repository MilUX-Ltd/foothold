# Thinking traps

Read this when Step 6 or Step 7 of `SKILL.md` applies. Raise only the traps that plausibly fit the decision in hand, one at a time. For each: say what it is, how it might be showing up here, and ask one question that tests it.

## Where these ideas come from

Most of the list grew out of the "heuristics and biases" research of Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman from the early 1970s. A heuristic is a mental shortcut: a quick rule of thumb that usually works and saves effort. A bias is the predictable error a shortcut produces in particular conditions. Their point was not that people are foolish, but that the errors are systematic, so they can be anticipated and checked. Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011) is the readable overview; he received the Nobel memorial prize in economics in 2002 for this work (Tversky had died in 1996).

A fair caution: some findings in this field have replicated less well than others, and lab effects are often smaller in real decisions where people have experience and feedback. The traps below are among the better established. Use them as prompts to look again, not as diagnoses of the user.

## Sunk cost

**What it is.** Letting money, time or effort already spent push you to carry on, when only future costs and benefits should count. The spent money is gone whichever option you choose.

**Origin.** Economists have long said sunk costs should be ignored. Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer (1985) showed in a series of experiments that people do not ignore them: having paid more for something made people more likely to keep using it, even when a better alternative was available.

**How it shows up in a small business.** "We've spent £20,000 on this system, we can't drop it now." Keeping a poorly performing hire because of the recruitment cost.

**Test question.** "If you were starting fresh today, with nothing spent, would you choose this?"

## Anchoring

**What it is.** The first number you hear, even an irrelevant one, pulls your later estimate towards it. You adjust away from the anchor, but not far enough.

**Origin.** Tversky and Kahneman (1974) asked people to estimate a quantity after spinning what looked like a random wheel; estimates leaned towards the number the wheel showed.

**How it shows up.** A supplier's first quote sets the range for negotiation. Last year's price sets this year's, regardless of costs. A salary expectation from the candidate frames the offer.

**Test question.** "Where did that first figure come from, and what would you estimate if you had never heard it?"

## Confirmation bias

**What it is.** Looking for, noticing and believing evidence that agrees with what you already think, and discounting what does not.

**Origin.** Peter Wason's "2-4-6" task (1960) found that people tested their guess about a number rule only with examples that would confirm it, rarely with examples that could prove it wrong. Raymond Nickerson's 1998 review drew together decades of evidence that the tendency is widespread.

**How it shows up.** Asking only enthusiastic customers whether they would pay more. Reading the one good reference for a candidate and skimming the lukewarm one.

**Test question.** "What would you expect to see if this were the wrong choice, and have you looked for it?"

## Overconfidence

**What it is.** Being more certain than the evidence allows. It shows up most in forecasts of time, cost and sales, and in ranges that are too narrow.

**Origin.** Calibration studies, reviewed by Sarah Lichtenstein, Baruch Fischhoff and Lawrence Phillips (1982), found that when people say they are 90 per cent sure, they are right far less often than that. Kahneman and Tversky (1979) described the related planning fallacy: plans are built on best-case scenarios, so projects run late and over budget.

**How it shows up.** "The new hire will be up to speed in a month." "The price rise won't lose us anyone."

**Test question.** "Give me a range you'd be surprised to fall outside. How did the last similar project actually go?"

## Status quo bias

**What it is.** Preferring things as they are because any change feels like a loss, even when the change is better on balance.

**Origin.** William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser (1988) showed that people chose an option much more often when it was presented as the current state. It links to loss aversion in Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory (1979): losses weigh more heavily than equal gains.

**How it shows up.** Staying with a supplier or bank out of habit. Not raising prices for years. Note the opposite risk too: an owner keen on change can undervalue "do nothing".

**Test question.** "If you were already doing the alternative, would you switch to what you do now?"

## The pre-mortem

**What it is.** Before deciding, imagine it is a year on and the decision has failed, then explain why. It gives everyone permission to voice doubts without seeming disloyal.

**Origin.** Gary Klein, a psychologist who studies how experienced people make decisions in the field, described the method in *Harvard Business Review* (2007). It draws on work by Deborah Mitchell, J. Edward Russo and Nancy Pennington (1989), who found that "prospective hindsight", imagining an event has already happened, increased people's ability to generate reasons for outcomes.

**How to run it.** Ask for three to five reasons for failure. For each: how likely, how bad, can it be prevented, and what early sign would show it happening? Carry the early signs into the decision note.
